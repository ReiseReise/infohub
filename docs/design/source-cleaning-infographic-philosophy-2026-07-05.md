# Cartographic Signal

Cartographic Signal treats information overload as terrain. The visual language is not a dashboard full of numbers, but a carefully surveyed map where dense areas, quiet zones, danger marks, and protected routes are all visible at once. Space carries hierarchy: the center holds the retained signal, outer rings hold sources that should be slowed, quarantined, or retired. Every shape should feel placed after careful measurement, not decoration.

Color works as governance rather than mood. Deep teal marks durable signal, blue marks useful but capped flow, amber marks watchful downgrade, and red marks sources that should be paused. The palette is restrained, calibrated, and meticulously crafted so that a viewer can understand the system before reading any label. Text is sparse and decisive: short source names, counts, and action verbs only.

The composition should feel like a master-level operations map: part editorial desk, part observatory, part logistics chart. It should reward close reading through small reference marks, proportional bands, and compact lists, but remain readable at a glance. The work must look labored over with painstaking attention, as if every gap, weight, and alignment was refined until the information became calm.

The final expression should avoid ornamental clutter. The artifact must demonstrate that cleaning sources is not deletion; it is cultivation. Strong sources are protected, loud sources are throttled, broken sources are rested, and exploratory sources are contained. This visual philosophy turns source governance into a clear field of action.
